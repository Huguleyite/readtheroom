◆  This week: The White House warns that investigating the president could be a crime. Democrats say investigating the president is the job.  ◆

Democrats do not take control of the House until January, and the fight over congressional oversight has already started. On Monday, Nov. 9, White House counsel Will Scharf sent a letter to Rep. Robert Garcia of California, the top Democrat on the House Oversight Committee and its incoming chair. The letter warns that Democratic plans to investigate the administration could cross into “blatant criminality.” Garcia answered that the investigations will go ahead.

What happened

The letter cites two Supreme Court cases, Watkins v. United States (1957) and Trump v. Mazars (2020), for one principle: congressional investigations need a legitimate legislative purpose. It then quotes Democrats’ own campaign-season remarks, including one lawmaker’s post about handing out subpoenas “like candy.” Its argument is that threats against officials, or subpoenas meant to frighten rather than inform, are not oversight. ABC reports that the letter says unlawful subpoenas meant to deprive people of constitutional rights “may warrant further investigation.”

The letter names no statute and makes no explicit demand.

Two days earlier, on Nov. 7, President Trump posted on Truth Social that the Justice Department should look into Democrats he accused of threatening administration officials and Republican donors. He called them “extortionists.” The Justice Department has not publicly confirmed any investigation.

On Election Day, Garcia told anyone who has abused the American public to “lawyer up.” Responding to Scharf, he said the Constitution gives Congress “wide latitude to conduct investigations,” and that the president appears to have something to hide. Democrats have named four areas of focus: Trump and his family, immigration enforcement, corporate corruption, and Jeffrey Epstein. The White House has long denied wrongdoing.

The case for the White House

Congress can investigate in order to legislate. It cannot investigate to punish, and the Supreme Court has said so. The incoming majority has talked about subpoenas as a prize, and the letter argues that language shows intent. A warning sent before the first subpoena is cheaper than a lawsuit sent after it.

The case for Democrats

Article I gives Congress the investigative power, and Garcia says it does not require the White House’s permission. Investigating the executive branch is the standard function of an opposition majority. History offers a comparison: after Republicans won the House in 2022, they investigated the Biden family and Anthony Fauci, and followed through on much of it.

Others in the room

Rep. James Comer, the Kentucky Republican who chairs Oversight now, said the committee is “very independent” and called “lawyer up” bad taste. Rep. Thomas Massie, a Kentucky Republican who often breaks with Trump, said he hopes for real hearings about real things, and that there is “some real corruption” around the fringes. Attorney General Todd Blanche said there “could be” a crime if rhetoric became threatening, but also said Congress has oversight authority and always will.

Peter Shane, a constitutional law professor at NYU, told The New York Times that the Constitution protects legislators from the threat of criminal prosecution while they do official work. He added that a subpoena that gets quashed is not a criminal offense.

How these fights end

Rarely in a courtroom verdict, and almost never in a cell. Congress’s own jail power fell out of use by the 1930s. The criminal contempt statute leaves prosecution to the executive branch, and since 1984 the Justice Department has said it will not prosecute executive officials who invoke executive privilege. A 2020 Congressional Research Service review found that the House had cited at least six executive officials since 2008, with no action on any referral. Civil lawsuits have worked better, but slowly. The House won in district court in the Miers and Holder cases, and both settled. The Mazars fight over Trump’s financial records ran from 2019 into 2022 and ended in a settlement, after a federal appeals court narrowed the subpoena. Both sides know this. That is partly why the warnings are loud.

What to watch

1. January. Who gets the first subpoenas, and whether they come from the four named areas.

2. The Justice Department. Whether anything follows Trump’s Nov. 7 request, or whether the letter is the whole of it.

3. Privilege and the courts. Whether the administration refuses the first subpoenas, claims executive privilege, and forces a lawsuit. Until then, Comer’s Republican-run committee holds the gavel.

The bottom line

The two sides have served notice on each other. In January, the subpoenas will be served too, and a judge will probably have the last word on who has to answer.
