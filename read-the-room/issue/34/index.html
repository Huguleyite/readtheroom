Three of a Kind: The Trump-Xi-Putin Summit

On Thursday, Nov. 19, in Shenzhen, President Trump sat down with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin. It was the first time the three have met in one room, and the Trump-Xi-Putin summit lasted a little over an hour on the sidelines of the APEC gathering. They left with a four-point declaration and no numbers. Whether that counts as progress depends on what you think talking is worth.

What happened

The three leaders met at Kylin Villa. Trump also met Xi and Putin separately the same day. Afterward, Xinhua and the Kremlin each published the full text of a joint “Declaration Toward Peace.”

It makes four commitments. The three will open a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing proliferation, and they affirm that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought. They back unspecified reform of the United Nations. They pledge to expand the supply of refined petroleum products and stabilize energy flows. And they endorse immunity for state officials and oppose what they call abuse of International Criminal Court jurisdiction.

The text does not mention Ukraine, the Iran war, or Taiwan. It commits no country to cut a single warhead.

The case for the summit

Supporters start with the principle that adversaries with nuclear weapons should be in contact. Trump made that case afterward: “No peace was ever forged by declining opportunities for engagement and speech.” He also said his relationships with Xi and Putin keep the country safe.

They also point to what was missing before Thursday. Rep. Rob Wittman, a Virginia Republican, noted that neither Russia nor China has a nuclear arms reduction treaty with the United States, and said he was glad they were willing to have the conversation. Sen. Rick Scott, a Florida Republican, described the approach before the summit as building relationships so the countries do not end up at war, even as he called Xi and Putin liars.

Observers had doubted that China would agree to nuclear talks at all. Beijing’s signature on a line about nuclear risk is, on this view, a door opening. The ICC language also matches existing administration policy, which has included sanctions on ICC officials.

The case against the summit

Critics say the summit produced a picture and a paragraph. Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer called it a “historic embarrassment” and “a glorified photo op and a gift to Putin and Xi.” Rep. Gregory Meeks of New York, the top Democrat on House Foreign Affairs, said he wished Trump had a plan and a strategy, and added that apparently he does not.

The sharpest objection concerns who was absent. Ukraine and its allies were not at the table. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said a Yalta format will not bring peace. More than 40 European leaders and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy met in Berlin the same day, and some coverage dubbed the Shenzhen summit “Yalta 2.0.”

Critics also read the declaration for what it avoids. The ICC clause, they note, shields leaders including Putin, who faces an ICC arrest warrant.

Others in the room

Sen. Tim Kaine, a Virginia Democrat, went in with high hopes but said he was nervous about any discussion of Ukraine that does not include the Ukrainians. Europe’s reaction was a counter-meeting rather than a statement. Both parties include members who want the talks to succeed and members who doubt the three leaders can be trusted to keep them. Few argue the meeting itself was dangerous. The dispute is about price.

What it does and doesn’t change

The practical stakes sit in an expired treaty. New START, the last arms limit between Washington and Moscow, ran out in February 2026, and nothing has replaced it. Trump says he wants a “new, improved and modernized” deal that includes China.

The numbers explain why China matters. The United States and Russia each hold more than 5,000 warheads. China holds more than 600. The Pentagon projects that China will pass 1,000 by 2030.

A dialogue is not a treaty. The declaration names no negotiators, no schedule, and no limits. Its value depends entirely on what follows.

What to watch

1. The G20 in Florida in December. Trump and Xi have one more meeting planned there. A follow-up on nuclear talks would show the declaration has legs.

2. Names, dates, delegations. Watch for whether the nuclear dialogue gets any of them. Without those, it stays a sentence.

3. A seat for Ukraine and Europe. Watch for whether either gets into the next round of talks, or whether the format stays closed.

The bottom line

Agreeing to talk is the easy part, and all three have now done it. The next test is whether the talks produce a number.
