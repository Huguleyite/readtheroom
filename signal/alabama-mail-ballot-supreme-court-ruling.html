The Supreme Court Just Kept Your Absentee Ballot Running on Alabama's Rules, Not the Postal Service's

Chambers and Lee County voters have had absentee ballots in hand since September 9. The Supreme Court just made sure the rules for delivering them didn't change five weeks before Election Day.

September 2026  ·  The Frequency Signal  ·  Alabama Politics

By the Numbers
  • Ruling date: September 14, 2026 — Supreme Court denies the Trump administration's emergency stay request
  • Vote: Only Justices Alito and Thomas dissented; Justice Kavanaugh's concurrence on timing was the decisive opinion
  • What stays blocked: USPS's voter-list portal and mandatory ballot-envelope barcodes, for the 2026 election only
  • Who sued: 23 Democratic-led states plus D.C. — Alabama was not among them
  • Alabama absentee voting: Began September 9, 2026; every ballot still travels through the U.S. Postal Service
  • Alabama deadlines: Applications due Oct. 27 (mail) / Oct. 29 (in person); ballots due back by noon, Nov. 3

Chambers and Lee County voters have had absentee ballots in hand since September 9. On September 14, the U.S. Supreme Court made sure the rules governing how those ballots get delivered didn't change out from under them five weeks before Election Day.

The Court refused to let the Trump administration enforce new U.S. Postal Service rules on mail ballots before November. It's a national ruling. Alabama's absentee system is different enough from other states' mail voting that it's worth being precise about what actually changed here, and what didn't.

What the Rule Would Have Done

In March 2026, President Trump signed an executive order directing the Postal Service to restrict delivery of mail ballots to voters not on a preapproved state list. USPS spent the following months turning that into policy, issuing final rules in late August that would have required states to submit voter lists through a new federal online portal and put unique barcodes on ballot return envelopes.

Twenty-three states, all Democratic-led, sued along with several voting rights groups. Alabama wasn't one of them. Federal courts sided with the challengers, finding the rule was likely an unconstitutional reach into a power the Constitution leaves to the states, and blocked it from taking effect while the case played out. The Trump administration went to the Supreme Court to lift that block in time for this fall's elections.

What the Court Actually Decided

On September 14, the justices said no. The unsigned order found the government "unlikely to succeed on the merits" of its case. Only Justices Alito and Thomas dissented; Alito argued USPS has broad authority to regulate the mail and that the challengers didn't have standing to sue in the first place.

The more telling opinion came from Justice Kavanaugh, who didn't dispute that the Postal Service might have the underlying authority to write a rule like this. He concurred with blocking it anyway, on a narrower ground: state and local election officials "do not have sufficient time to reasonably implement the rule" this close to an election. That's a timing objection, not a verdict on the merits. The broader legal fight over whether USPS can do this at all stays open for future elections. For November 2026, mail ballots move under the rules that were already in place.

Where Alabama Actually Fits

Alabama doesn't have mail-in voting the way Trump's order pictured it. Secretary of State Wes Allen has been direct about that distinction: Alabama offers absentee ballots only to voters with a qualifying excuse, such as college students, active-duty military members, and caregivers, and requires a photo ID with the application. "There is no early voting period where voters are allowed to mail in their ballots without submitting a valid absentee ballot application," Allen has said.

That narrower system doesn't put Alabama outside this ruling's reach. Every one of those absentee ballots still travels through the U.S. Postal Service, which means Alabama's process would have been swept into the same barcode-and-portal requirements as any state with no-excuse mail voting. The Alabama NAACP's Shalela Dowdy put the objection plainly: "Why would we try to make it harder for people to vote when being able to vote by mail provides everyone to have access to the ballot?"

For Chambers and Lee County voters, the practical calendar hasn't moved. Absentee applications are due by mail on October 27 and in person by October 29. Completed ballots returned by mail are due back by noon on Election Day. That's the same day Hazel Floyd and Kristin Nelson face each other again for House District 38, and Lee McInnis takes on Mike Rogers for Alabama's 3rd Congressional District.

What's Still Unsettled

This was an emergency order, not a final ruling on whether a president can direct the Postal Service to restrict mail ballot delivery. Kavanaugh's concurrence leaves that door open for a future Congress, court, or election cycle with more lead time to work with. Nothing about this decision is permanent. What it guarantees is narrower, and for this November, sufficient: the system Chambers and Lee County election officials already know how to run is the system they'll be running on November 3.
